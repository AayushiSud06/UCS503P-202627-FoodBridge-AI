\section{Background and Context}
\label{sec:background}

Institutional kitchens routinely finish a service with edible food left over.
College messes, hostel canteens, bakeries and banquet halls are typical
sources. At the same time, shelters, community kitchens and non-governmental
organisations (NGOs) a few kilometres away are short of meals. Food waste is
a recognised problem at every scale~\cite{unepFoodWaste2024}. For a single
campus or a tier-2/3 Indian city, however, the binding constraint is rarely
the \emph{supply} of surplus food or the \emph{existence} of organisations
willing to serve it. It is \emph{coordination under a deadline}. Cooked food
is safe to serve for only a few hours, and the phone calls and informal
messages that currently connect a donor to a recipient often outlast that
window.

FoodLink is the software system this project builds to close that gap. It is
a web platform that brings four kinds of participant into one shared,
tracked workflow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \textbf{donor}, who posts surplus food with a pickup location and an
    absolute pickup deadline;
  \item a \textbf{recipient organisation} (an NGO or community kitchen),
    which is verified by an administrator before it can take custody of food;
  \item a \textbf{volunteer courier}, who collects the food and delivers it;
  \item a \textbf{platform administrator}, who vouches for organisations,
    maintains the platform and can act on behalf of any party.
\end{itemize}

The project is being developed for the course UCS503P (Software Engineering
Project), 2026--27 odd semester, at Thapar Institute of Engineering and
Technology. The course asks for three reports: a proposal in week~4, a
prototype-stage progress report before the mid-semester examinations in
week~7, and a final report in week~17~\cite{ucs503Project}. This document is
the mid-term (prototype-stage) report. The proposal committed to a
web-based, workflow-centred platform with a transparent ranking
heuristic~\cite{foodlinkProposal}. This report describes the system that has
actually been built against that proposal, how it was verified, and what
remains.

\paragraph{A note on terminology.} The repository is named
\emph{FoodBridge-AI}, and the product was at one point labelled
``FoodLink AI''. FoodLink contains \textbf{no machine learning, deep learning
or computer vision}. Recipient organisations are ranked by a transparent
weighted sum of five published criteria, which a reader can recompute by hand
(Section~\ref{sec:module-matching}). This is a deliberate design decision
recorded in the project's decision log as D-05~\cite{foodlinkArchitecture}.
Every technical statement in this report was checked against the source code
at commit \texttt{e588b35}~\cite{foodlinkRepo}. Where the code and the
project's own documentation disagree, the code was followed.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Surplus food is lost less for lack of food or willing recipients than for
lack of a reliable, timely and trustworthy way to connect them. Five
concrete problems follow from informal coordination:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A hard perishability deadline.} Food has a window after which
    it must be discarded. Coordination that is not deadline-aware routinely
    misses it.
  \item \textbf{Fragmentation.} A donor contacts whichever organisation it
    happens to know. There is no shared pool, so availability is invisible to
    everyone else, and the nearest suitable organisation may never hear of the
    food.
  \item \textbf{No accountability or evidence.} Nobody can say afterwards how
    long it took for food to be claimed, whether it was actually delivered, or
    how much was lost. Without records, the process cannot be measured or
    improved.
  \item \textbf{Last-mile coordination.} Food must be physically moved.
    Volunteer availability is informal and untracked, so a willing donor and a
    willing recipient can still fail to complete a handover.
  \item \textbf{Trust and privacy.} A donor handing food to a stranger needs
    assurance that a real organisation stands behind the request. A donor who
    may be an individual at home should not have their exact location
    broadcast to anyone who signs up.
\end{enumerate}

The problem addressed by this project is therefore to \emph{design and
implement a web-based platform that lets a donor post surplus food with a
hard pickup deadline, directs it to nearby verified organisations through an
explainable ranking, coordinates a volunteer courier through pickup and
delivery, and records every step as server-stamped evidence, while ensuring
that each participant can see and do only what its role and its relationship
to a donation permit.}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Four observations motivate the design.

\textbf{Coordination, not supply, is the bottleneck.} Surplus food and
recipient organisations already exist. A modest reduction in the delay
between posting and acceptance directly converts food that would have been
discarded into served meals. This is why the proposal's primary evaluation
metric is \emph{time-to-claim}~\cite{foodlinkProposal}, and why the system
treats the pickup deadline as an absolute timestamp rather than a display
string.

\textbf{Trust must be earned, not asserted.} Anyone can register as an
organisation. If self-registration were enough to receive food, the platform
would hand food, and donors' addresses, to unvetted accounts. FoodLink
therefore separates \emph{holding a role} from \emph{being trusted}. An
administrator verifies each organisation, and that verification gates
ranking, acceptance and access to the open donation pool.

\textbf{Decisions should be explainable.} A recipient deciding whether to
accept, a donor wondering why an organisation was suggested, and an
evaluator checking the system should all be able to see \emph{why} a ranking
came out as it did. A published weighted sum with per-criterion scores and
plain-language reasons meets that requirement. An opaque learned model
would not, and would in any case need outcome data the project does not yet
have.

\textbf{Evidence should not be self-reported.} Metrics such as
time-to-claim and rescue rate are only credible if their timestamps come from
the server at the moment of each transition. FoodLink records every lifecycle
change in an append-only ledger (\code{status_events}) and derives its metrics
from it.

As a software engineering course project, the work also aims to demonstrate
sound engineering practice: explicit requirements, a documented architecture
and decision log, automated tests for every security and integrity property,
and continuous integration.

\subsection{Proposed System}
\label{sec:proposed}

FoodLink is a three-tier web application. A React single-page application
talks to a REST API written in Python with FastAPI, which persists data
through SQLAlchemy to a relational database (SQLite by default). The API
is the single authority for every rule. The browser renders what the API
permits and never decides access or lifecycle legality itself.

The end-to-end flow, as implemented, is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \textbf{donor posts} a donation: food name, category, quantity and
    unit, storage type, description, an optional photograph, the pickup
    address and map coordinates, an optional preparation time and an absolute
    pickup deadline. The server rejects a deadline that has already passed.
  \item The server stores the donation as \code{AVAILABLE}, stamps an
    \code{AVAILABLE} event, and immediately \textbf{ranks} every recipient
    organisation. An organisation is eligible only if it is verified, has map
    coordinates and lies within an 8\,km service radius. If any is eligible,
    the donation moves to \code{MATCHED}, and the leading score is frozen on
    the donation. A match is a suggestion: nothing is assigned.
  \item Every \textbf{verified organisation} sees the donation in its open
    pool while the deadline has not passed, together with its own live
    match score and explanation. One organisation \textbf{accepts}, and the
    donation becomes \code{ACCEPTED}.
  \item The donation appears in the unclaimed queue of every
    \textbf{volunteer courier}. There the pickup location is shown only as a
    coarse area of roughly one square kilometre. One courier
    \textbf{claims} it (\code{VOLUNTEER_ASSIGNED}) and only then sees the exact
    pin, address and donor name.
  \item The courier \textbf{collects} the food (\code{PICKED_UP}) and
    \textbf{delivers} it (\code{DELIVERED}).
  \item The accepting organisation \textbf{confirms receipt}
    (\code{COMPLETED}). The organisation's completion count and the courier's
    delivery count are incremented, which in turn feeds the reliability
    criterion of future rankings.
  \item Along the way, the donor (before pickup) or an administrator may
    \textbf{cancel}, the accepting organisation may \textbf{release} a claimed
    pickup back to the courier queue, and an administrator may run the
    \textbf{expiry sweep} that closes overdue, unaccepted donations as
    \code{EXPIRED}. Every transition appends a server-stamped event.
\end{enumerate}

Six principles run through the implementation and recur throughout this
report: the server is the only authority; lifecycle rules are data rather
than scattered conditionals; rankings are explainable; trust comes from
administrator verification; each reader is told only what its relationship
to a record justifies; and history is append-only.

\subsection{Scope}
\label{sec:scope}

\textbf{In scope and implemented at mid-term.} Account registration and
sign-in for the donor, organisation and courier roles, and command-line and
administrator-only creation of administrators. Role-, ownership-, lifecycle-
and verification-based authorization. Donation posting with a
browser-resized photograph. Explainable recipient ranking. The full
nine-state donation lifecycle, with conditional (race-safe) status writes and
an append-only event ledger. Courier claim, pickup and delivery. Receipt
confirmation. Standing requirements posted by organisations, and a donor
needs board. Organisation verification. The manual expiry sweep. Platform
metrics. Four role-specific web portals. Rate limiting of sign-in,
registration and donation posting. Automated backend and frontend test
suites run in continuous integration.

\textbf{Partially implemented.} Cancellation, pickup release and
administrator account management are implemented and tested in the API but
are not yet offered in the web portal. The same applies to an organisation
setting its map coordinates, which it needs in order to be ranked. Phone-layout
screens exist under the \code{/m/*} routes but are reachable only by typing
the address.

\textbf{Out of scope, or not yet built.} Email, SMS or push notifications;
maps, road distances or route planning; object storage for photographs (they
are stored inline in the database row); a scheduler for the expiry sweep; any
deployment or hosting; a real-world pilot with users; machine learning or
computer vision; and any certification of food safety. As the proposal
states, the platform coordinates handovers and records the information a
recipient needs; it does not vouch for the food~\cite{foodlinkProposal}.

\section{Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Objective 1:} Allow a donor to post surplus food with an
    absolute pickup deadline, validated and stored by the server, within a
    single short form.
  \item \textbf{Objective 2:} Rank eligible recipient organisations for each
    donation with a transparent, published weighted heuristic whose
    per-criterion scores and reasons are returned with every result.
  \item \textbf{Objective 3:} Enforce a complete donation lifecycle, from
    posting to completion, cancellation or expiry, in which illegal
    transitions and concurrent conflicting transitions are refused, and every
    transition is recorded as a server-stamped event.
  \item \textbf{Objective 4:} Restrict every read and write by role,
    ownership, lifecycle state and administrator-granted trust, and withhold
    sensitive data (such as a donor's exact pickup location) from readers who
    do not need it.
  \item \textbf{Objective 5:} Make demand visible before supply by letting
    organisations post standing requirements that donors can browse.
  \item \textbf{Objective 6:} Derive the proposal's evaluation metrics
    (median time-to-claim, rescue rate, expiry-loss rate and median handover
    time) from the event ledger rather than from self-reported data.
  \item \textbf{Objective 7:} Protect the implementation with automated tests
    and continuous integration, so that every security and integrity property
    is exercised on each change.
  \item \textbf{Objective 8:} Prepare the system for deployment and a pilot
    with real donors, organisations and couriers.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Organisation of the Report}
\label{sec:organisation}

The report follows the chapter structure of the TIET report
template~\cite{tietTemplate} and adds two chapters that this evaluation
requires.
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} presents the methodology, architecture,
requirements, database, API and frontend design, and then documents the
implementation module by module. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} describes how the
system was tested and what the results show, including its current
limitations. Chapter~\ref{ch:feasibility} is the separate Feasibility Report,
covering technical, operational, economic, schedule, and legal, security and
privacy feasibility. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} summarises the work, the key
findings and future work. Chapter~\ref{ch:uml} closes the report with the UML
design artefacts: the use case diagram with detailed use case scenarios, the
class diagram and the activity diagram.
